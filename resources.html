01

Why We Walk Away

The longer you study a deal, the easier it becomes to want the work to produce a transaction. Here’s why that instinct is exactly what Denali is built to override.

Read the Full PieceClose

Capital allocation requires the ability to say no.

There is a psychological risk in every acquisition. The longer you study a deal, the easier it becomes to want the work to produce a transaction. Time has been spent. Relationships have been built. A business plan begins to feel familiar.

None of that makes the investment better.

Todd believes one of the most important responsibilities in capital allocation is preserving the ability to walk away — particularly after significant time and effort have already been invested.

That does not mean every decision will produce the desired outcome. Real estate carries risk. Conditions change. Information is imperfect.

“I want to be able to explain why we made the decision even if the outcome is disappointing.” — Todd W. Michael

That is the personal side of Denali’s underwriting discipline. The objective is not certainty. It is a decision that was reasonable when it was made — based on assumptions that could be defended, risks that were understood, and a structure that left room for conditions to change.

A decision should remain defensible after enthusiasm disappears.

02

What Investors Deserve to Know Before They Commit

Investors should understand the decision before they’re asked to share the risk. Here’s the standard Denali holds itself to — before capital moves, and after.

Read the Full PieceClose

Investors should understand the decision before they are asked to share the risk.

Capital stewardship is not only what happens in the underwriting model. It is also how the decision is explained.

Denali’s responsibility is to communicate what the investment depends on, which assumptions matter, where the risks are, what ownership can control, and what it cannot. Investors should have room to ask difficult questions before capital is committed — not discover the important ones afterward.

That standard does not end at acquisition. When conditions change, communication matters more, not less. Good news is easy to report. Stewardship becomes visible when the news is complicated.

Todd’s objective is straightforward: investors should understand how Denali arrived at a decision, what management is watching, and why the response makes sense.

Trust should follow the work. It should not be asked to substitute for it.

03

How Denali Executes and Protects the Business Plan

Buying well is half the job. Here’s what happens after closing — how the plan gets executed, and why reserves are underwritten before they’re ever needed.

Read the Full PieceClose

Value should be created through execution — not assumed into existence.

Denali looks for operating improvements that can be identified, executed, and measured. Occupancy, collections, unit turns, maintenance, staffing, utilities, resident retention, vendor performance, and capital planning are where the business plan becomes real.

Targeted renovations and ancillary income can add value, but the plan should not require every initiative to work immediately. Operational upside should create opportunity — not stand between the investment and failure.

Reserves are part of the investment.

Roofs age. HVAC systems fail. Plumbing breaks. Pavement deteriorates. Residents move. Insurance events occur. Renovation schedules change. Those are not exceptions to the business plan. They are part of owning real estate.

Denali evaluates known capital needs before acquisition and maintains liquidity for the things that cannot be scheduled perfectly.

Capital sitting in reserve may not make a projection look more exciting. It can make the investment more survivable.

04

What Breaks the Deal — and What Happens Then

Denali’s first underwriting question, answered in full — the stress test, the failure points, and why the exit is never the thesis.

Read the Full PieceClose

Ask what breaks the deal before asking how good it can get.

Denali tests the investment under conditions less favorable than the base case: weaker rent growth, lower occupancy, higher expenses, slower execution, capital overruns, unfavorable financing conditions, and wider exit cap rates.

The purpose is not to predict every possible outcome. It is to locate the failure points.

What happens to debt coverage? How much liquidity remains? Can the business plan slow down? Does the property still have operating options? Does the investment require a refinance or sale at exactly the right time?

A strong investment does not need to be invulnerable. It needs to give ownership room to respond.

“I want to be able to explain why we made the decision even if the outcome is disappointing.” — Todd W. Michael

The exit should be an option, not the thesis.

A Denali investment should not depend on selling at the perfect moment. Hold, refinance, or sell decisions should be driven by the property’s performance, the capital structure, market conditions, investor economics, and the alternatives available at the time.

The more an investment depends on a perfect exit, the less margin for error it has during the years that come before it.

We do not need the future buyer to rescue today’s underwriting.
